Groundwater Levels on the Delmarva Peninsula

Understanding shallow groundwater in an extremely flat, coastal landscape

Why groundwater?

Shallow groundwater on the Delmarva Peninsula can vary substantially over short distances. In this extremely low-relief coastal landscape, groundwater frequently approaches the land surface, influencing agriculture, drainage, ecosystems, and the performance of septic systems.

These local differences are difficult to capture with conventional hydrologic models. Small variations in elevation, soil properties, vegetation, drainage infrastructure, and groundwater gradients can strongly influence where water accumulates and where it drains.

The goal of this work is to develop high-resolution groundwater predictions across the Delmarva Peninsula and use them to identify areas where groundwater hazards may create the greatest environmental and societal challenges.

Mapping groundwater at high resolution

High-resolution predictions allow us to capture spatial variability that may be missed by coarser regional groundwater models. This is particularly important in flat coastal environments, where local features can strongly influence groundwater levels.

Adapting RHESSys for a flat coastal landscape

The Regional Hydro-Ecologic Simulation System (RHESSys) is a physically based, spatially distributed hydrologic model that simulates interactions among water, vegetation, soils, and energy across landscapes.

RHESSys was originally developed for landscapes where topography provides strong controls on water movement. Those assumptions are less appropriate on the Delmarva Peninsula, where elevation gradients are extremely small and groundwater can move independently of surface watershed boundaries.

We therefore adapted RHESSys to better represent the processes that control shallow groundwater in this landscape.

Drainage ditches

High-resolution mapping of agricultural drainage ditches allows the model to represent their role in draining saturated soils and connecting different parts of the landscape.

Local groundwater gradients

Local groundwater gradients are incorporated rather than relying solely on broad-scale topographic gradients to drive groundwater flow.

Cross-watershed flow

Groundwater can move across surface watershed boundaries. The adapted model allows these connections to be represented.

Spatial soils

Spatial variation in soil properties influences infiltration, storage, drainage, and groundwater recharge.

Land cover & vegetation

Different land covers and vegetation conditions influence evapotranspiration, recharge, and drainage.

Local infrastructure

Features such as agricultural ponds and drainage infrastructure are incorporated to better represent local hydrologic processes.

From process-based modeling to peninsula-wide predictions

RHESSys is first used to simulate groundwater dynamics in monitored watersheds. These simulations provide physically based predictions while preserving relationships among environmental conditions and groundwater levels. A machine learning model is then trained using the same environmental inputs and RHESSys predictions, allowing groundwater estimates to be extended across the entire peninsula.

Connecting groundwater to septic vulnerability

Groundwater hazards become particularly important when they overlap with infrastructure that depends on subsurface conditions.

Septic systems are one example. When groundwater levels are very shallow, there may be less unsaturated soil available for wastewater treatment and disposal. Understanding where shallow groundwater and septic systems overlap can therefore help identify areas that may warrant additional attention.

From environmental hazards to community vulnerability

Groundwater conditions alone do not determine vulnerability. The consequences of groundwater hazards depend on the infrastructure, properties, and communities located in affected areas.

To better identify areas where adaptation may be most needed, groundwater predictions can be combined with socioeconomic indicators, parcel-level property values, and septic system locations.

A targeted approach

Combining environmental hazards with socioeconomic information helps identify communities where groundwater challenges may create the greatest environmental and societal impacts.

A scalable framework for coastal groundwater assessment

This framework combines physically based hydrologic modeling, machine learning, high-resolution environmental data, and socioeconomic information to move from understanding groundwater processes to identifying actionable areas of vulnerability.


The resulting maps can help support more targeted planning and allocation of adaptation resources in communities facing increasingly challenging groundwater conditions.
